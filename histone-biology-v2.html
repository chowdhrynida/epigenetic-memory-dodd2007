How does a cell remember which genes are on?

Stochastic simulation of histone-based epigenetic memory: a study inspired by Dodd et al. (2007)

Internship project, Physical Biology Lab, IIT Bombay
Janhavi Yadav and Nida Chowdhry, MSc year 1, SIES College

Every cell in your body carries the same DNA, yet a neuron and a skin cell behave very differently. Before the simulation, we build the biology from the ground up. What you see on the right is one nucleosome, the unit everything in this project is built on.

Start with the biology

Two metres of DNA in one tiny nucleus

Stretched out, the DNA of a single human cell is about two metres long. The nucleus that holds it is only a few micrometres wide.

What you see is 240 base pairs, less than 0.1 micrometre of that thread. The double helix is about 2 nm wide, and every base pair adds 0.34 nm of length. The rungs are the base pairs.

  • A–T pair
  • G–C pair
  • sugar-phosphate backbone

So there are two problems to solve: pack all of it, and still be able to read the genes that are needed.

Histones are the spools

DNA is packed by winding it around proteins called histones. There are four kinds, and they come in pairs: eight proteins make one histone octamer.

  • H3 ×2
  • H4 ×2
  • H2A ×2
  • H2B ×2

Histones are rich in lysine and arginine, which carry a positive charge. The sugar-phosphate backbone of DNA is negative. Opposite charges attract, and that is what makes the DNA stick to the spool.

147 base pairs, 1.65 turns

DNA winds around the octamer in a left-handed superhelix, 147 base pairs long and about 1.65 turns. The result is a nucleosome, a disc roughly 11 nm across.

The DNA that runs from one nucleosome to the next is called linker DNA. Drag the slider and feed the DNA onto the spool yourself.

Beads on a string

Nucleosomes repeat along the DNA, joined by linker DNA. This is the classic beads-on-a-string picture of chromatin.

The nucleosome is the unit that the model in this project follows. It simulates a row of 60 of them, and each one is in exactly one of three states.

  • Uunmodified
  • Aacetylated
  • Mmethylated

Here we show 7 nucleosomes, all unmodified. The gene is poised: neither strongly on nor off.

The tails are where the switches sit

Each histone has a flexible tail that sticks out between the two turns of DNA. Along the tail are lysines (K), each with a positive charge that can grab the DNA.

Enzymes can attach small chemical groups to these lysines. The name H3K9 means lysine number 9 on histone H3. Click or tap a lysine in the model to change its mark: none, acetyl, then methyl.

  • unmodified lysine (+)
  • acetyl
  • methyl

Acetylation: the gene switches on

A histone acetyltransferase (HAT) adds an acetyl group to a lysine. That removes the positive charge, so the tail lets go of the DNA and the nucleosome grips its neighbours less.

Chromatin opens, and RNA polymerase can reach the gene and start making RNA. Watch the tails first, then the gene region.

Typical marks at active genes: H3K9ac, H3K14ac, H3K27ac.

Methylation: the gene switches off

A histone methyltransferase adds methyl groups instead. A methyl group does not remove the charge. What matters is the protein that reads it.

In fission yeast, Clr4 methylates H3K9 and Swi6 (an HP1 protein) binds to it. Swi6 bridges neighbouring nucleosomes and packs the region tight. The polymerase cannot get in, and the gene is silenced.

Not every methyl mark silences: H3K4me3 sits at active genes. It is H3K9 and H3K27 methylation that go with repression.

Marks are written, erased, and copied

Writers add marks, erasers remove them, readers bind them. When a modified nucleosome recruits enzymes that put the same mark on its neighbours, the state spreads and reinforces itself. That is a feedback loop.

The same lysine can carry an acetyl or a methyl, never both, so the two states compete. This is the core question of the project: can simple feedback plus random noise give a stable, heritable state?

Each tick, one nucleosome either copies the state of a random other one (feedback) or changes at random (noise). Watch the gene follow the majority.
