Stochastic simulation of histone-based epigenetic memory

A study inspired by Dodd et al. (2007). Part 1 is the biology: how DNA is spooled onto histones and how two chemical marks switch a gene on or off. Part 2 is the model: a kinetic Monte Carlo simulation of feedback and noise, and what it takes to make a chromatin state bistable and heritable.

Internship project, Physical Biology Lab, IIT Bombay. Nida Chowdhry, MSc 1st year, SIES College.

Start with the biology Jump to the model

Two metres of DNA fit inside one tiny nucleus

A human cell holds about 2 m of DNA in a nucleus roughly 10 µm across. Folding it is not just storage: how tightly a stretch is folded decides whether its genes can be read.

DNA is a twisted ladder with a negative charge

Two strands wind into a right-handed double helix. They run in opposite directions (5′ to 3′ and 3′ to 5′) and are held together by base pairs: A with T, G with C.

One full turn takes about 10.5 base pairs, or 3.6 nm. The sugar-phosphate backbone carries a negative charge. Keep that in mind: it is the reason DNA sticks to histones.

Histones: eight proteins that form one spool

Four kinds of histone (H2A, H2B, H3, H4) come in pairs. Together they form a disc called the histone octamer.

Histones are rich in positively charged amino acids, so the negatively charged DNA is drawn to them. Each histone also has a flexible tail that sticks out of the disc. Those tails carry the chemical marks this project is about.

DNA winds around the spool: that is the nucleosome

Negative DNA meets positive histones. The DNA wraps 1.65 times around the octamer in a left-handed superhelix.

That is 147 base pairs per nucleosome, and it makes the DNA about seven times shorter along its length. Short stretches of linker DNA join one nucleosome to the next.

The tail carries the switches

Lysines (K) on a histone tail are positively charged, so they grip the negatively charged DNA. Enzymes add or remove small chemical tags on them.

Acetyl (Ac) cancels a lysine's + charge, the grip weakens and the tail lets go. Methyl (Me) keeps the charge but creates a docking site for reader proteins such as Swi6 (HP1 in humans).

Acetylation opens a gene, methylation closes it

Put the marks on a stretch of eight nucleosomes and watch the whole fibre change shape.

Acetylated nucleosomes loosen and the gene becomes readable. Methylated nucleosomes recruit readers, clump together and hide the gene. A reporter gene such as ura4+ tells the experimenter which way it went.

Next: let the nucleosomes flip at random

In the simulation each nucleosome is in one of three states: methylated (M), unmodified (U) or acetylated (A). With some probability a nucleosome copies the state of another one, and otherwise it changes at random.

A region of N = 60 nucleosomes. The question: can simple feedback plus random noise give a stable, heritable state?

Why fission yeast, and why a feedback loop?

The project is inspired by silencing at the mating-type region of S. pombe, read out with a reporter gene, ura4+, inserted into that region.

Silencing there is associated with H3K9 methylation, the reader protein Swi6, and the enzymes Clr4 (writer) and Clr3/Clr6 (erasers). The key biological fact this project turns into a model: a modified nucleosome can recruit the enzymes that put the same modification on its neighbours. That closes a feedback loop.

Core question: can simple feedback, plus random noise, produce chromatin states that are stable and heritable — without invoking anything more exotic?

Model overview: a kinetic Monte Carlo simulation

A region of N = 60 nucleosomes, each in one of three states — Methylated (M), Unmodified (U) or Acetylated (A) — is updated one nucleosome at a time using the Gillespie-style kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) method.

Every step, one nucleosome n1 is picked at random. With probability a it copies the state of another randomly chosen nucleosome n2 (recruited, feedback-driven conversion — and nothing happens if n2 is U). Otherwise, with probability 1−a, n1 converts to one of the other two states at random (noise).

F = a / (1−a)the feedback-to-noise ratio: how much recruitment dominates over random flipping

Bistability as a function of F

Sweep F upward and something qualitative changes: the system stops hovering around a mixed state and starts committing to one of two extremes.

At low F (0.4, 1.0) the count of M nucleosomes just fluctuates around M ≈ 20 — the noise term dominates and there is no clear state. At higher F (1.4, 2.0), two distinct states emerge — a high-M state and a low-M (high-A) state — and the system jumps between them rather than sitting in the middle.

Bistability in nucleosome modification only appears once feedback overcomes noise.

Left: M(t) over 5000 attempted conversions per nucleosome, for four F values. Right: the corresponding probability distribution of M over the run. At F = 0.4–1.0 the distribution is a single broad hump; by F = 1.4–2.0 it is visibly two-peaked, and the time trace shows the system living in one peak for long stretches before flipping.

How long does a state last, and how clean is it?

Two summary statistics track bistability as F sweeps from 0.1 to ~4: how long the system stays committed to one state, and how clearly it favours one mark over the other.

Lifetime vs F. A "high-M" state is defined as M > 1.5×A (and symmetrically for "high-A"). Lifetime is how long the system stays continuously in one such state. At low F, lifetime is short — the state switches often. At high F it grows sharply (note the log scale), meaning strong feedback doesn't just make one state more likely, it makes it last far longer once reached: this is the model's version of epigenetic memory.

Gap score vs F. G = |M−A| / (M+A) measures how strongly the system favours one mark. At low F, G sits near 0 (a mixed state); as F rises, G climbs toward 1 (clear dominance by one mark), confirming the same bistable transition seen above from a different angle.

Lifetime vs F — log-log. Lifetime is flat and short for F below about 1, then rises steeply, spanning almost four orders of magnitude by F ≈ 1.5–2.
Gap score vs F. G rises from about 0.15 at low F to almost 1 by F = 4, with the steepest climb between F = 1 and F = 2 — the same crossover seen in the lifetime plot and in the bistability histograms.

Need for cooperativity

Feedback alone does not always guarantee bistability. Cooperativity means an enzyme is only recruited when more than one nucleosome nearby already carries the mark — it adds non-linearity, which is what lets the system commit to a clear "on" or "off" decision instead of drifting.

Two flavours show up in the model: implicit cooperativity, which arises naturally because going from A to M (or back) is a two-step reaction through U; and explicit cooperativity, where the rules are changed so that recruitment requires two nucleosomes in the same state before it can fire. The three cases below isolate which reactions need that explicit boost.

Live, running the model's own Case B rule at F = 77 on both rows (only modification is ever recruited — losing a mark only happens by noise): the top row lets one randomly-picked nucleosome decide a recruitment on its own (non-cooperative) and stays mixed, hovering near zero net mark. The bottom row only recruits when two independently, randomly-picked nucleosomes already agree (cooperative) — watch it commit to one mark and stay there, exactly the sharp, switch-like behaviour that shows up as a steep G vs F curve in the real result below.
Gap score G vs F, cooperative (solid) vs non-cooperative (dashed), for all three cases below. Case A: the two curves lie almost on top of each other. Case B: cooperative rises far earlier than non-cooperative. Case C: both stay low, cooperative only modestly higher.

Case A · Modifying & demodifying recruited

Both the M←U/A←U step and its reverse use recruited feedback. Coop. and non-coop. curves are almost identical, and P(M−A) is clearly two-peaked for both.

Implicit cooperativity is already enough — bistability is strong with or without explicit cooperativity.

Case B · Only modifying recruited

Only U→A and U→M use recruitment. The cooperative curve is bimodal; the non-cooperative one is essentially flat across M−A — it never commits to a state.

Explicit cooperativity is essential here: with only one reaction recruited, the system needs it to become bistable.

Case C · Only demodifying recruited

Only A→U and M→U use recruitment. Non-coop. gives one broad hump centred at zero; coop. gives two modest peaks around ±40 — better, but still not sharp.

Explicit cooperativity for demodification alone does not meaningfully rescue bistability.

Need for beyond-neighbour interaction

So far, any nucleosome could recruit any other nucleosome in the region — no spatial limit. Real chromatin folds in 3D: some distant nucleosomes are brought close together, while some near ones along the DNA are pushed apart. So: what happens if recruitment range is restricted?

Three spatial rules are compared: no constraint (fully connected), neighbour-only (a nucleosome can recruit only its immediate neighbours), and a power-law contact model, where recruitment probability falls off with genomic distance as P ∝ 1/|i−j|1.5 — a simple stand-in for 3D chromatin contact frequency.

Case A · Standard model (no constraint)

Any nucleosome can contact any other with equal probability, however far apart along the DNA.

Indigo arcs fire uniformly at random, short and long alike — a mark set near one end can jump straight to the far end in a single step.

Case B · Neighbour-limited model

A nucleosome can only contact its immediate left/right neighbour — nothing further.

Only short, adjacent arcs ever light up. A mark can only creep outward one nucleosome at a time, so it takes far longer — if it happens at all — to cross the whole row.

Case C · Power-law contact model

Contact probability decays with genomic distance as P ∝ 1/|i−j|1.5 — mostly short-range, but occasionally a long-range pair like nucleosome 1 and nucleosome 44 still contacts directly, and the formula below shows exactly how that probability was worked out each time.

P(i → j) ∝ 1/|i−j|1.5  ·  example: |1−44| = 43 → P ∝ 1/431.5 ≈ 0.0035
Gap score G vs F for the three spatial rules. Case A rises steepest and earliest; Case C rises almost as early but a little more gradually; Case B needs F an order of magnitude larger before G climbs, and even then rises less cleanly.

Case A · Standard (fully connected)

Any nucleosome can recruit any other. Gap score is high and P(M−A) is clearly bimodal even at moderate F.

A fully connected system maintains memory easily.

Case B · Neighbour-limited

Recruitment reaches only immediate neighbours. Even at F = 6–26, P(M−A) stays broad and roughly flat around zero; only at very high F does a trace of two-peakedness appear at the edges.

Local-only recruitment is not enough for memory across the whole region.

Case C · Power-law contact

Recruitment probability decays as 1/|i−j|1.5, mimicking long-range 3D contacts. P(M−A) becomes bimodal by moderate F, though the peaks are broader than in Case A.

Spatially extended but distance-weighted feedback partially restores memory.

Key insights & conclusion

1Positive feedback alone is not enough to guarantee bistability — cooperativity is essential in several of the tested cases.
2Epigenetic states can be highly dynamic at the level of individual nucleosomes, yet stable overall at the level of the whole region.
3Non-local nucleosome interaction is critical: purely local, neighbour-only recruitment fails to sustain memory.
4Nucleosome-modifying enzymes may need to bind and act on more than one nucleosome at a time to reproduce the observed bistability.

The simulation reproduced the theoretical model and its results matched Dodd et al.'s published findings, demonstrating the core principles of epigenetic memory from a minimal set of rules: recruited feedback, random noise, cooperativity and spatial range.
